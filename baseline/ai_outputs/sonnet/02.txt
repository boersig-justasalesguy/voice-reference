Top Strategic Technology Trends to Plan for in 2027

Every year, technology leaders face the same problem: far more promising ideas than time, money or talent. A strategic trend list is useful only if it helps you decide what to fund, what to pilot and what to ignore. The seven trends below are the ones we believe will most affect enterprise planning in the coming year. They are grouped into three themes: building trust, scaling intelligence and reshaping the foundation.

Theme 1: Building trust

1. AI governance platforms

As AI spreads into products and workflows, organizations need a way to inventory models, assess risk, enforce policy and show evidence to regulators. Governance platforms bring these tasks together. Planning implication: treat governance as an operating function with an owner and budget, not a policy document. Start with an inventory of every AI system in use, including those embedded in purchased software.

2. Disinformation security

Synthetic audio, video and text have made impersonation cheap. Fraud against executives, support desks and payment workflows is growing. Organizations will need tools and processes to verify identity and content provenance. Planning implication: review high-value approval workflows and remove any that depend on a voice or video call as the only proof of identity.

3. Preemptive cybersecurity

Defenders are moving from detecting attacks to disrupting them before they land, using exposure management, automated remediation and deception. Planning implication: shift part of your security budget from alert handling to reducing attack surface, and measure the change in exploitable exposure rather than the count of alerts.

Theme 2: Scaling intelligence

4. Agentic AI

Software agents that plan, call tools and complete multistep tasks are moving from demonstrations to production. They promise productivity gains, but they also introduce new risks: unintended actions, excessive access and unclear accountability. Planning implication: begin with narrow, well-bounded use cases, grant agents the minimum permissions they need and require a human checkpoint for any action that is costly to reverse.

5. Domain-specific models

General-purpose models are powerful but expensive and often imprecise on specialized work. Smaller models tuned for a field such as legal review, claims processing or industrial maintenance can deliver better accuracy at lower cost and with easier compliance. Planning implication: evaluate whether your highest-volume AI use cases would be served better by a specialized model, and invest in the proprietary data that makes tuning possible.

Theme 3: Reshaping the foundation

6. Hybrid computing

No single processing approach fits every workload. Organizations are combining conventional cloud, specialized accelerators, edge computing and, for narrow problems, early quantum and neuromorphic systems. Planning implication: build workload placement skills and avoid long commitments to one architecture while the hardware landscape is changing quickly.

7. Sustainable technology operations

Energy demand from AI and data centers has made power a strategic constraint. Customers and regulators are also asking for credible emissions data. Planning implication: add energy and carbon cost to your technology architecture decisions, and ask vendors for measured, not estimated, figures.

How to use this list

Do not try to act on all seven. We suggest a simple filter:

- Relevance: Does this trend touch a business priority you already have?
- Readiness: Do you have the data, skills and funding to start in the next twelve months?
- Risk: What is the cost of being late, and what is the cost of being wrong?

Trends that score high on all three deserve investment now. Those that score high on relevance but low on readiness deserve a small pilot and a plan to close the gap. The rest should be monitored, with a named person responsible for revisiting them in six months.

The common thread is that technology choices are now inseparable from questions of trust, accountability and resilience. Organizations that plan for those questions early will move faster, not slower, when the technology matures.
